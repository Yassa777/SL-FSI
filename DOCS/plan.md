Research Proposal: Financial Stress Regime Detection in Sri Lanka Using Hidden Markov Models
 
1. Research Topic
Detecting and characterizing financial stress regimes in Sri Lanka using Hidden Markov Models applied to mixed-frequency macro-financial data, with the aim of producing a transparent, reproducible early-warning framework for small, open emerging markets.
 
2. Background of the Research
Sri Lanka's 2022 sovereign default exposed a critical gap in financial surveillance: the absence of a shared, transparent mechanism for distinguishing temporary stress from structural regime shifts. This gap contributed to policy decisions that, in retrospect, reflected misdiagnosis of the economy's operating state.
On January 18, 2022, the Central Bank of Sri Lanka repaid a $500 million International Sovereign Bond maturity—a "business as usual" decision predicated on the belief that foreign exchange pressure was manageable and that maintaining repayment reputation justified depleting scarce reserves. Eighty-four days later, on April 12, 2022, Sri Lanka suspended external debt payments, explicitly acknowledging that reserves were needed for essential imports and that debt service had become unsustainable.
The interval between these two decisions represents a regime transition that was not made explicit in time. Had a transparent stress indicator signaled that the system had already shifted from "pressure" to "crisis" by late 2021, policymakers could have initiated creditor engagement earlier, moved to exchange-rate flexibility in a more orderly fashion, and conserved foreign exchange for essential imports rather than debt service.
This is not a uniquely Sri Lankan problem. Import-dependent emerging markets routinely face the challenge of distinguishing short-lived external shocks (oil price spikes, temporary capital outflows) from structural imbalances (chronic current account deficits, unsustainable debt trajectories). The policy response appropriate for each is fundamentally different: temporary shocks warrant reserve drawdowns and short-term controls; structural imbalances require adjustment. Misclassification leads to reserve depletion, distortionary controls, and eventual forced adjustment under crisis conditions.
Despite the importance of this distinction, most financial stress indices and regime-switching frameworks are built for developed markets with deep, liquid financial systems and high-frequency, synchronized data. Emerging markets—particularly small ones—face mixed-frequency data, gaps, measurement error, and thin markets that violate the assumptions of standard approaches. The Central Bank of Sri Lanka publishes a Financial Stress Index in its annual Financial Stability Review, but the methodology is not public, the underlying data are not accessible, and independent researchers cannot replicate or extend it.
This research addresses a dual gap: the practical need for a transparent, reproducible stress regime indicator for Sri Lanka, and the methodological need for a documented approach to regime detection that works under emerging-market data constraints.
 
3. Research Aim
To construct a transparent, reproducible regime-classification framework for Sri Lanka's financial stress using mixed-frequency macro-financial data and probabilistic regime-switching methods, producing dated regime probabilities that can serve as a foundation for early-warning diagnostics in small, open emerging markets.
 
4. Research Approach / Objectives
Methodological Approach
The research employs Gaussian Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) to infer latent stress regimes from observed financial and macroeconomic indicators. HMMs are suited to this problem because financial stress exhibits four properties that the model explicitly captures:
1.	Latent structure: Stress is not directly observed; we observe symptoms (FX moves, yield spreads, reserve losses, inflation) that are generated by an underlying state.
2.	Discrete, persistent modes: The economy operates in distinct regimes (calm, pressure, crisis) rather than along a continuous stress gradient. Once a regime shift occurs, the system tends to remain in the new state.
3.	Regime-dependent distributions: The statistical behavior of indicators changes across regimes—volatility, means, and correlations are state-dependent.
4.	Noisy observations: Day-to-day variation is high; the model must filter signal from noise.
Alternative approaches were considered and rejected for specific reasons. Principal Component Analysis captures correlation structure but assumes a stable covariance matrix and does not model regime switches. Weighted composite indices produce smooth scores that obscure discrete transitions. Threshold models are brittle to noise—a single spike can trigger false alarms. Machine learning classifiers can achieve high accuracy but function as black boxes, undermining interpretability and trust for policy applications.
More sophisticated regime-switching models, particularly Markov-Switching Vector Autoregression (MS-VAR), were considered but deferred. MS-VAR requires dense, synchronized multivariate time series to estimate regime-specific dynamics reliably. Sri Lankan data—characterized by mixed frequencies, forward-filling, and gaps—would risk overparameterization. Gaussian HMM is a deliberate stage-one model: robust to data limitations, less prone to overfitting, and capable of producing interpretable regime labels that can later be enriched with dynamic structure.
Research Objectives
Objective 1: Data harmonization and feature engineering Merge daily market data (FX, interbank rates, equity indices, turnover, sovereign yields) with monthly macroeconomic data (reserves, inflation, tourism earnings, remittances) into a unified panel. Construct derived stress features including FX and equity returns, rolling volatilities, real interest rates, reserve dynamics, and sovereign spread proxies. Document coverage rates and missingness patterns.
Objective 2: Regime identification Fit Gaussian HMMs with 2–4 latent states on a core, high-coverage feature set. Characterize each inferred regime by its typical indicator values (mean FX volatility, reserve trajectory, spread levels). Estimate transition probabilities to quantify regime persistence and asymmetric transition patterns.
Objective 3: Event-alignment validation Evaluate whether inferred regime switches align with documented crisis milestones using a pre-specified event list and dual evaluation windows:
●	Tactical window (±14 days): appropriate for market/liquidity actions
●	Strategic window (±60 days): appropriate for policy sequencing decisions
Define "hit" as a sustained crossing (regime probability ≥ τ for at least K consecutive days) within the relevant window. Evaluate using cost-weighted scoring with asymmetric loss (λ > 1 for missed signals relative to false alarms), reflecting that failing to detect a crisis is more costly than a false alarm. Benchmark HMM performance against a simple z-score composite threshold model.
Objective 4: Minimal indicator set identification Conduct feature ablation experiments to identify the smallest, highest-coverage subset of indicators that reproduces regime timelines with acceptable stability. This addresses both practical concerns (a parsimonious model is more usable) and methodological concerns (results should not depend on idiosyncratic indicator choices).
Objective 5: Robustness and sensitivity analysis Test stability across specifications: alternate regime counts (2, 3, 4 states), alternate feature subsets, different scaling choices, and different sample windows. Report regime probabilities rather than hard labels to communicate uncertainty.
 
5. Research Questions
RQ1: Can Sri Lankan macro-financial data from 2018 to 2025 be parsimoniously explained by a small set of persistent stress regimes, and what are the observable signatures (typical FX volatility, reserve dynamics, sovereign risk levels) that characterize each regime?
RQ2: Do HMM-inferred regime transitions align with documented crisis milestones from the 2021–2023 period, and do regime probabilities shift before public headline events—providing evidence of early-warning potential?
RQ3: What is the minimal, highest-coverage set of indicators that reproduces the same regime timeline with reasonable stability across specifications?
 
6. Research Outcomes
Expected Contributions
Empirical contribution: A dated, probabilistic stress-regime history for Sri Lanka (2018–2025), including transition dates, regime durations, and quantitative characterization of each state. Before this research, analysts could describe the 2021–2022 crisis narratively but could not point to a probability-based timeline of when the system entered and exited distinct stress states. After this research, they can reference a reproducible regime history and say: "The system entered high stress at this date, remained there for this duration, and these indicators defined it."
Methodological contribution: A documented workflow for regime detection in data-scarce emerging markets, addressing mixed-frequency data integration, feature selection under missingness, and event-alignment evaluation using pre-registered windows and cost-weighted error metrics. Before this research, applying regime-switching methods to mixed-frequency, gap-filled emerging-market data felt ad hoc. After this research, analysts can follow a defensible procedure that others can scrutinize and replicate.
Practical contribution: An open data pipeline and visualization dashboard that ingests Sri Lanka macro-financial series, computes features, estimates regimes, and outputs regime probabilities and change-point diagnostics. Before this research, even believers in "stress regimes" could not run an end-to-end system that others could audit, reproduce, or extend. After this research, they can.

Feasibility
The project draws on a merged Sri Lanka macro-financial panel covering 2018–2025 at daily and monthly frequencies, constructed from 18 core data streams (FX rates, policy and market interest rates, equity indices and turnover, official reserves, inflation, tourism earnings, worker remittances, and global anchors including gold prices and US Treasury yields) plus derived stress features (returns, volatilities, spreads, real rates, reserve metrics).
Data are sourced from the Central Bank of Sri Lanka's public statistical tables, the Colombo Stock Exchange, and global market data providers (Yahoo Finance for gold and US yields). The pipeline is documented and reproducible; others can regenerate the merged panel from source files.
Preliminary exploratory work—including initial HMM fits and Bayesian change-point detection—suggests that the data support distinguishable regime states that broadly correspond to known crisis periods. A working Streamlit dashboard already visualizes regime probabilities and change points, demonstrating that the core infrastructure is operational. The remaining work is systematic evaluation, robustness testing, and documentation—not greenfield development.
 
7. Plans for Sustainability
The research outputs are designed to persist and remain useful beyond the initial study:
Open repository: All code, data pipelines, and documentation will be published in a public GitHub repository under a permissive license. This enables independent verification, extension, and adaptation to other country contexts.
Modular data pipeline: The data ingestion and merging scripts are designed for periodic updates. As new CBSL statistical releases become available, the pipeline can regenerate updated panels with minimal manual intervention.
Dashboard as living tool: The Streamlit visualization dashboard can be hosted publicly (via Streamlit Cloud or similar) and updated as new data arrive. This provides ongoing value to researchers and analysts monitoring Sri Lankan financial conditions, independent of whether the original research team continues active development.
Documentation for transferability: The methodological contribution—the workflow for mixed-frequency regime detection—will be documented at a level of detail sufficient for researchers to apply the approach to other emerging markets with similar data constraints. The Sri Lanka case serves as a worked example; the method is intended to be portable.
 
8. Plans for Commercialisation
The primary orientation of this research is academic and public-interest: producing open, reproducible tools for financial surveillance in a context (small emerging markets) that is underserved by existing commercial offerings.
However, several pathways to applied or commercial use exist:
Advisory and consulting services: The regime-detection framework and dashboard could support consulting engagements with financial institutions, multilateral organizations, or government agencies seeking to implement or adapt early-warning systems. The open methodology provides credibility; customization and interpretation provide value-added services.
Subscription-based monitoring: A productized version of the dashboard—offering real-time regime probability updates, alerting on regime transitions, and cross-country comparisons—could serve institutional investors, risk managers, or development finance institutions with exposure to frontier and emerging markets. The Sri Lanka implementation would serve as proof-of-concept for a broader emerging-market stress monitoring product.
Capacity building and training: The documented workflow could form the basis for training programs or technical assistance engagements, helping central banks or finance ministries in other emerging markets build internal regime-monitoring capabilities using their own data.
These commercialisation pathways are secondary to the research contribution itself. The core outputs—regime history, methodology, and open pipeline—will remain publicly available regardless of any downstream commercial applications.
